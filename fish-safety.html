Florida Keys Fish Safety

“Poisonous” and “venomous” are not the same. Some Keys fish can carry toxins when eaten; others inject venom through spines or barbs.

Toxic to eat

Poisoning happens after eating

Ciguatoxin, tetrodotoxin and histamine can cause serious illness. Several are not destroyed by cooking or freezing.

Venomous to handle

Venom is delivered by a spine or barb

Lionfish, scorpionfish, stingrays and saltwater catfish can cause painful puncture wounds. Their flesh is not automatically poisonous.

Fish to avoid or treat with extra caution

Do not eat

Pufferfish and porcupinefish

They can contain tetrodotoxin, a potent neurotoxin concentrated in organs and skin. Cooking, freezing, smoking or pickling does not make it safe.

Practical Keys rule: do not clean or eat a locally caught pufferfish.

Highest ciguatera concern

Great barracuda

CDC guidance identifies barracuda as a high-risk ciguatera fish and advises never eating it. The toxin cannot be seen, smelled or tasted and survives cooking and freezing.

Possible ciguatera

Large reef predators

Grouper, amberjack and snapper can accumulate ciguatoxin. Risk generally increases with a fish’s size and position in the food chain.

  • Ask local captains about current problem reefs.
  • Avoid the head, liver, intestines and roe.
  • CDC recommends avoiding tropical reef fish over roughly five pounds.
Improper chilling

Mahi, tuna and amberjack

These are not naturally poisonous, but poor temperature control can produce scombroid histamine poisoning. Ice them immediately and keep them below 38°F.

Venomous fish you may encounter

Venomous spines

Lionfish

Up to 18 dorsal, pelvic and anal spines can sting even after the fish is dead and on ice. The fillets are edible after the spines are safely controlled.

Venomous spines

Scorpionfish

Excellent camouflage makes them easy to grab accidentally. Do not handle an unidentified bottom fish with bare hands.

Venomous barb

Stingrays

Shuffle your feet in shallow water and keep clear of the tail when releasing one. The tail barb can cause a deep, contaminated wound.

Fin spines

Hardhead and gafftopsail catfish

Dorsal and pectoral spines can puncture hands and inject venom. Use a dehooker or secure tool instead of gripping the fish.

Lionfish sting first aid: Immerse the wound in hot—but not scalding—water for 30–90 minutes. Seek medical attention for severe pain, weakness, dizziness, breathing problems or a deep puncture.

If someone may have fish poisoning

Call 911 for breathing trouble, paralysis, collapse, severe weakness or rapidly worsening symptoms. In the United States, call Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222. Save a sample of the fish safely for investigators if possible.

Sources: CDC Yellow Book: Food Poisoning from Marine Toxins and FWC Lionfish Safe Handling.
