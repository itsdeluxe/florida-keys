Five outfits cover nearly every trip

Reel size numbers are approximate and vary by manufacturer. Match the rod’s printed line and lure rating to the line class—not only the reel number. Move toward the heavier end near bridges, wrecks, strong current or large fish.

1Light flats and reef

Rod7–7½ ft medium
Reel3000–4000 spinning
Main line15–20 lb braid
Leader20–30 lb fluorocarbon

Best for: pompano, yellowtail, mangrove snapper, hogfish, small tripletail and casting shrimp or jigs.

2All-around Keys

Rod7 ft medium-heavy
Reel5000–6000 spinning
Main line30–40 lb braid
Leader30–60 lb fluorocarbon

Best for: permit, mutton snapper, mahi, cobia, blackfin tuna and medium barracuda.

3Heavy reef and wreck

Rod6–7 ft heavy
Reel8000–14000 or 30–50 conventional
Main line65–80 lb braid
Leader80–120 lb mono/fluoro

Best for: black grouper, greater amberjack and extracting fish before they reach structure.

4Big tarpon

Rod7–8 ft heavy
Reel6000–10000 spinning
Main line50–65 lb braid
Leader60–100 lb mono/fluoro

Best for: adult tarpon at bridges, channels and passes. Lighter tackle is suitable for juveniles.

5Offshore trolling

Rod6–7 ft heavy trolling
Reel20–50 class conventional
Main line30–50 lb mono or 50–80 lb braid
Leader60–200 lb, species dependent

Best for: sailfish, mahi, blackfin and wahoo. Add 80–130 lb wire for wahoo.

If buying only one setup: a 7 ft medium-heavy spinning rod, quality 5000–6000 reel, 30 lb braid and spools of 30, 40 and 60 lb leader cover the broadest range of Marathon fishing.

Choosing main line and leader

MaterialUse it forWhat to know
BraidMost spinning and vertical-jigging setupsThin diameter, long casts and high sensitivity. It has little stretch, so use a mono or fluorocarbon leader and a smooth drag.
MonofilamentTrolling, shock leaders and beginner-friendly reelsStretch cushions surges and knots easily. It is thicker and less sensitive than braid.
FluorocarbonLeader around clear water and wary fishLow visibility and abrasion resistance. Stiffer and more expensive; inspect after every fish or reef contact.
WireWahoo and barracuda bite protectionUse the shortest practical section. Wire reduces bite-offs but can reduce bites from line-shy fish.
Leader length: Start with 2–4 ft for casting, 4–8 ft for clear-water live bait, and a longer shock/wind-on leader for trolling. Increase strength around bridge pilings and wrecks; decrease it when clear water makes fish reluctant.

Hooks, sizes and bait placement

Hook size is not standardized across brands. Choose the smallest hook with enough gap to clear the bait and the fish’s jaw, then confirm that the point remains fully exposed.

Florida reef-fishing default

Use a non-stainless-steel, non-offset (inline) circle hook with natural bait when targeting snapper, grouper, amberjack, hogfish and other reef fish. Keep a dehooking device and a descending device or venting tool rigged and ready. Exact gear requirements change across Atlantic/Gulf and state/federal boundaries, but this hook is the safest all-around Keys choice.

Circle hooks

Best for live or dead natural bait and fish likely to be released.

  • Do not make a hard hook-set.
  • Let the fish move off, point the rod toward it, reel steadily and lift once the line is tight.
  • Leave the point and gap exposed; do not bury the hook in the bait.

J-hooks and lure hooks

Useful for artificial lures, jigs and some trolling rigs where an active hook-set is part of the technique.

  • Avoid stainless steel when release is possible.
  • Replace damaged trebles with strong single hooks when practical.
  • Crimping the barb makes release faster and safer.

Starting hook sizes

  • #1–2/0: yellowtail, small snapper and shrimp.
  • 2/0–5/0: permit, tripletail and mangrove snapper.
  • 4/0–8/0: mahi, mutton, cobia and tuna.
  • 8/0–11/0: grouper, amberjack and large tarpon.

Hooking live bait

  • Shrimp: through the horn ahead of the dark organs, or lightly through the tail.
  • Crab: through a rear shell corner near a swimmer leg.
  • Baitfish: through both nostrils for current, or lightly ahead of the dorsal fin for free-lining.
  • Cut bait: once through a firm edge with hook point exposed.

Four knots, ranked by usefulness

The Palomar is the best first knot for most anglers, but no single knot wins every job. Use the quick chooser below, then follow the matching four-picture sequence—on a phone, swipe the pictures sideways. Tie slowly, keep wraps neat, moisten mono or fluorocarbon before cinching, and retie anything crossed, burned or uneven.

#1 PalomarBest overall and best with braid #2 UniBest versatile leader-to-hook knot #3 Double UniBest easy braid-to-leader connection #4 Improved ClinchBest simple choice for light mono
#1 BEST OVERALL

Palomar — hook, lure or swivel

  1. Double 6–10 in. of line and pass the loop through the eye.
  2. Tie a loose overhand knot with the doubled line; keep the hook hanging below.
  3. Pass the entire hook or swivel through the large loop.
  4. Moisten, pull the standing line and tag together, then trim.

Why it ranks first: strong, quick and particularly dependable with braid. Make sure the doubled strands never cross.

#2 MOST VERSATILE

Uni — leader to hook, lure or swivel

  1. Pass the leader through the eye and double back to form a loop.
  2. Wrap the tag around both strands and through the loop 5–7 times.
  3. Moisten and pull the tag to form the knot.
  4. Pull the standing line to slide the knot firmly to the eye; trim.

Why it ranks second: it works across a wide range of mono and fluorocarbon sizes, including heavier leader where an improved clinch is a poor choice.

#3 BEST EASY CONNECTION

Double Uni — braid to leader

  1. Overlap braid and leader by about 8 in.
  2. With the braid tag, form a loop and make 7–10 wraps; snug it.
  3. With the leader tag, form a second loop and make 4–6 wraps; snug it.
  4. Moisten and pull both standing lines so the knots slide together; trim close.

Why it ranks third: it is the easiest dependable line-to-leader knot to learn. An FG knot is slimmer through rod guides and better for very different diameters, but takes more practice.

#4 LIGHT LINE SPECIALIST

Improved Clinch — light mono

  1. Pass 6 in. through the eye and wrap the tag around the standing line 5–7 times.
  2. Pass the tag through the small loop immediately above the eye.
  3. Pass it through the larger loop just created.
  4. Moisten and draw the coils down evenly; trim.

Why it ranks fourth: useful and familiar for light mono or fluorocarbon, but less versatile than the Uni. Do not rely on it for braid or leader over 30 lb.

Final check: pull every finished knot hard with protected hands before fishing it. Leave a small tag, inspect the first several feet of leader after every fish, and retie after abrasion or a hard impact.

Useful Keys rigs

Free-lined live bait

main line → leader → circle hook

  • Most natural presentation for pilchards, ballyhoo, crabs and shrimp.
  • Add only enough split shot to reach the target depth.

Fish-finder bottom rig

main line → sliding sinker → bead → swivel → 3–8 ft leader → circle hook

  • Lets a fish move with the bait before feeling the weight.
  • Good for mutton snapper, grouper and cobia near bottom.

Knocker rig

main line → egg sinker → swivel or hook → short leader

  • Compact and less prone to tangles near bridges and reef.
  • Use the lightest sinker that holds bottom.

Yellowtail chum rig

light main line → 15–25 lb fluorocarbon → #1–2/0 circle hook

  • Usually no weight; hide a tiny hook in a small bait chunk.
  • Feed line naturally at the same speed as the chum.

High-speed wahoo rig

main line → heavy shock leader → ball-bearing swivel → wire → lure

  • Use only components carrying a trolling-speed rating.
  • Rigging and drag should be checked by an experienced offshore angler.

Vertical jig setup

braid → 4–8 ft leader → solid ring/split ring → assist-hook jig

  • Good on humps and wrecks for tuna and amberjack.
  • Match jig weight to depth and current.

Terminal-tackle checklist

  • Inline, non-stainless circle hooks in several sizes
  • Jigs, bucktails and casting plugs
  • 20, 30, 40, 60, 80 and 100 lb leader
  • Wire leader and haywire-twist pliers for toothy fish
  • Egg sinkers, bank sinkers, beads and swivels
  • Dehooker and long-nose pliers
  • Line cutters suitable for braid and wire
  • Descending device or venting tool rigged and ready
  • Landing net plus release gloves
  • Measuring tape/device and current rules
